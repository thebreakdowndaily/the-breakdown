# The Breakdown — Evidence-Led Storytelling Playbook v1.0

**Status:** Editorial reference for commissioning, reporting, story architecture, generation and review  
**Purpose:** Make complex investigations compelling because the reporting is strong—not by dramatising, guessing or imitating another journalist.

## 1. The governing idea

A strong story is both a **reader journey** and an **evidence trail**. The reader should want to continue, understand how the central claim is supported, see the mechanism behind the event, and know what remains uncertain.

Do not treat “the world's best journalists” as an objective ranking. The journalists and projects below are selected as useful case studies because their work demonstrates identifiable methods, supported by award citations, craft interviews or newsroom standards. Learn the methods; do not copy distinctive phrasing, signature metaphors, sentence rhythms, or a living journalist's voice.

## 2. A selective craft shelf: work to study, method to adapt

### Patrick Radden Keefe — structure discovered while reporting
**Source:** [ProPublica interview, “Patrick Radden Keefe Gets to the Bottom of It”](https://www.propublica.org/article/patrick-radden-keefe-interview) (July 18, 2022)

**Transferable method**
- Think about structure during reporting, not only after the research is complete.
- Draft a small number of major “beats”: opening, turns, transitions, evidence reveals and destination.
- Choose an opening that creates a useful question or changes the reader's expectations; do not force a cinematic cold open.
- Integrate explanatory context where the reader needs it instead of interrupting the story with a large block of background.
- If the story promises to resolve a factual mystery, investigate enough to answer it—or state precisely why the evidence cannot settle it.

**Use at The Breakdown:** A beat sheet plus a “reader question answered by each section” table, revised as evidence changes.

### Hannah Dreier — individual lives reveal systemic failure
**Source:** [Pulitzer Prize, 2024 Investigative Reporting](https://www.pulitzer.org/prize-winners-by-category/206)

**Transferable method**
- Start with a verifiable human case when it gives the reader a concrete way into a complex system.
- Move deliberately from the individual's experience to the institutions, incentives and failures that produced or allowed it.
- Support systemic claims with records, multiple cases, data and accountability reporting; the opening character is not a substitute for proof.
- Give affected people agency and specific circumstances instead of using them as emotional props.

**Use at The Breakdown:** “One case → recurring pattern → mechanism → responsible institutions → documented response.”

### Azmat Khan and The New York Times team — granular cases plus aggregate evidence
**Source:** [Pulitzer Prize, 2022 International Reporting](https://www.pulitzer.org/winners/staff-new-york-times-notably-azmat-khan-contributing-writer)

**Transferable method**
- Combine documentary material, field reporting, local testimony and quantitative analysis.
- Examine individual cases closely, then analyse the cases together to test whether a larger pattern exists.
- Compare official accounts with evidence gathered independently.
- State the limits of the dataset, including missing cases, unclear denominators and what cannot be known.

**Use at The Breakdown:** Maintain both a case ledger and a pattern-level analysis. Do not describe an aggregate finding as universal if the evidence is incomplete or selection-biased.

### Declan Walsh and The New York Times team — follow money, resources and power across borders
**Source:** [Pulitzer Prize, 2025 International Reporting](https://www.pulitzer.org/prize-winners-by-year/2025)

**Transferable method**
- Connect local harm and lived consequences with the movement of money, commodities, institutions and external influence.
- Map the mechanism: actors, transactions, intermediaries, decisions, incentives and consequences.
- Use forensic evidence to substantiate the chain; proximity, association or a plausible geopolitical theory is not proof.

**Use at The Breakdown:** For conflicts, infrastructure, policy and business stories, build a documented flow map alongside the human and chronological narrative.

### John Hersey — restrained witness-centred narrative
**Source:** [The New Yorker archive, “Hiroshima”](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1946/08/31/hiroshima)

**Transferable method**
- Make an event of enormous scale comprehensible through carefully reported individual experiences.
- Let witnessed details and consequences carry meaning; avoid inserting the reporter's emotional verdict where evidence can speak for itself.
- Preserve chronology and the limits of what each witness could know.

**Use at The Breakdown:** Choose a limited number of well-sourced people or cases and return to them only when the next scene adds information. Never invent thoughts, dialogue, sensory details or continuity.

### Neha Dixit — accountability reporting at the intersection of power and vulnerable communities
**Source:** [Committee to Protect Journalists profile](https://cpj.org/awards/neha-dixit-india/)

**Transferable method**
- Ask who is absent from official narratives and whose experience the record obscures.
- Connect individual allegations to institutions and policy while preserving the distinction between allegation and established fact.
- Treat source safety, retaliation risk, contextual fairness and accurate description of reporting limits as part of the method—not post-publication considerations.

**Use at The Breakdown:** Add a “who is missing from the record?” question and source-harm review to assignments involving people at risk.

### P. Sainath and the People's Archive of Rural India — sustained attention to overlooked lives
**References:** [P. Sainath](https://www.psainath.com/) · [People's Archive of Rural India](https://ruralindiaonline.org/)

**Transferable method**
- Report from communities often marginalised by national news agendas, rather than describing them only through official statistics or elite sources.
- Preserve local knowledge, language, work, place and material conditions; show how broad forces are experienced on the ground.
- Treat context and continuity as essential. A community should not appear only when crisis makes it newsworthy.

**Use at The Breakdown:** For stories outside major metros or elite institutions, plan for local voices, geographic context and the everyday system around the headline.

### Reuters standards — the non-negotiable floor
**Source:** [Reuters Standards and Values](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)

**Method:** Accuracy before speed; fair comment; transparent corrections; honest source attribution; precise language; no fabrication or plagiarism; no misleading visual treatment.

**Use at The Breakdown:** Narrative quality never outranks verification, fair right of reply, source protection or correction duties.

## 3. Choose the narrative form to fit the reporting

Do not impose one structure on every assignment.

| Story type | Useful structure | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Breaking development | Most important verified facts first; timestamped updates; what remains unknown | Withholding the key fact for suspense |
| Investigation | Finding or verified scene → how the system works → evidence trail → response and counterevidence → consequences | Treating suspicion as the conclusion |
| Explainer | Reader's question → mechanism step by step → worked example → implications and limits | A glossary disguised as a story |
| Data story | Question → dataset and method → finding → human/institutional meaning → caveats | Charts without a finding or denominator |
| Profile | Specific reporting question → choices and constraints → evidence and contradiction → significance | Invented interiority or uncritical access |
| Historical analysis | What changed → chronology → competing explanations → source limits → relevance now | Present-day assumptions projected onto the past |
| Policy / business analysis | Decision → incentives and money flow → who gains/loses → implementation and accountability | Inferring intent from outcome alone |

A cold open, anecdote, timeline, chart, map or dramatic reveal is optional. Include one only when it improves comprehension and is supported by evidence.

## 4. The evidence-led narrative workflow

### Step 1 — State the reader contract
Before reporting or drafting, write:
- **Central question:** one precise question the story must answer.
- **Provisional thesis:** the strongest current explanation, explicitly provisional.
- **Reader promise:** what the reader will understand that they could not readily understand before.
- **Known / unknown:** key established facts, contested claims, and gaps.
- **Alternative hypotheses:** at least one plausible competing explanation.
- **Evidence that could change our mind:** documents, data or responses that would weaken the thesis.

Never make a conclusion a prerequisite for reporting.

### Step 2 — Build the evidence before arranging drama
Maintain a claim-to-source ledger. For each material claim record: claim ID; exact wording; claim type (fact, official statement, allegation, inference, opinion, forecast or unknown); supporting evidence; contradictory evidence; source independence; publication and access dates; limitations; confidence rationale; reviewer status.

The number of URLs is not the number of independent sources. A dozen stories repeating one wire report remain one reporting chain. A link is not proof that its page supports the claim.

### Step 3 — Write the beat sheet
Plan 5–9 possible beats, then revise them as the evidence develops. For every beat specify:
1. The question the reader has at this point.
2. The new verified information.
3. The evidence that supports it.
4. What it changes in the reader's understanding.
5. The transition or unanswered question that leads forward.
6. Any unresolved claim or fairness issue.

Beats are a planning aid, not a requirement to reach a predetermined ending.

### Step 4 — Use scenes only when reported
A scene is eligible only when its important details come from the reporter's direct observation, attributable interviews, contemporaneous notes, authenticated records, images/video whose origin and context have been checked, or a reconstruction explicitly labelled and authorised by editorial policy.

- Never invent dialogue, action, sensory detail, timing, motivation, memories or internal thoughts.
- Do not use a source's remembered scene as if the reporter witnessed it.
- Verify dates, location and sequence where they matter.
- If the detail cannot be verified, remove it, attribute it accurately, or state the uncertainty.
- Do not build composite characters or splice separate events into one scene without explicit, prominent disclosure and editorial approval. Default: do not use composites.

A vivid scene is a way into a story, not proof that the case is typical.

### Step 5 — Move between the person and the system
When the subject warrants it, use a two-lens structure:
- **Human lens:** who experienced the consequences, what choices and constraints they faced, what they can verify.
- **System lens:** rules, institutions, incentives, money, data, timelines, decision-makers and points of accountability.

Move between the two when the evidence adds explanatory value. Do not force a human anecdote into a story where it would be tokenistic or unsafe.

### Step 6 — Explain mechanism and causation
Separate sequence from cause. For causal claims, state the proposed mechanism and test alternatives. Ask:
- What decision or process links the cause to the outcome?
- What evidence shows that link?
- What other factors could explain the result?
- Is the claim based on correlation, a single case, a selected sample or a representative dataset?
- What evidence would contradict the explanation?

A chronology can establish what came before; it does not by itself prove why something happened.

### Step 7 — Integrate data as evidence, not decoration
Every number needs provenance, units, time period, denominator where relevant, and a sentence explaining its significance. Charts must use verified data supplied by an accessible source or documented dataset. If the data has not been collected or checked, label the chart **DATA REQUIRED** and show the intended question instead of inventing values.

State missingness, definitional changes, sampling limits and uncertainty. Never manufacture statistics to fill visual space.

### Step 8 — Give subjects a fair and accurately described opportunity to respond
For material allegations or criticism, record who was contacted, when, by what method, what was asked, the response or no-response, and any deadline. Do not imply outreach happened if it did not. Include a response in the correct context; do not use a quote to imply an answer to a question it did not address.

### Step 9 — End with understanding, not a slogan
A good ending may show what changed, what the evidence still cannot resolve, who has the next decision, what readers should monitor, or how the opening case now looks in light of the findings. The ending should add perspective or resolve the central question. Do not manufacture closure, prediction or emotional uplift.

## 5. Practical architecture for a reported investigation

Adapt as the evidence requires; omit modules that do not earn their place.

1. **Opening / central question** — a verified, revealing detail or a direct question. State the essential news promptly.
2. **Why it matters now** — the stakes for real people, institutions or public decisions.
3. **What the evidence shows** — the core finding, with the strongest records and the limits of the claim.
4. **How the mechanism works** — chronology, decisions, incentives, money and institutional relationships.
5. **The human consequences** — well-reported cases that illuminate, but do not overclaim, the pattern.
6. **Scale and comparison** — verified data, a transparent method and relevant baseline.
7. **The strongest counterevidence** — alternate explanations, uncertainties and material responses.
8. **Responsibility and remedies** — who had authority, what they did, what changed and what remains contested.
9. **What happens next / what remains unknown** — clearly separated forecasts, monitoring triggers and unresolved questions.
10. **Sources and methods** — primary documents, data, interview notes/description where safe, calculations, limitations and correction contact.

This is a menu, not a mandatory ten-part template. Structure should follow the reader's needs and the evidence's logic.

## 6. Headline and visual rules

- The headline must be a defensible summary of the evidence, not a promise the body cannot fulfil.
- Prefer specific actors, decisions and consequences over generic alarm words.
- Never state allegation as fact or infer motive without evidence.
- A hero image must accurately represent the subject or be clearly labelled as illustrative. Do not depict a real person doing an action that has not been verified.
- Captions must identify what is actually shown, where and when if known, and whether an image is illustrative, archival, supplied or generated.
- A generated illustration must never be presented as documentary evidence or as a photograph of a real event.
- Charts, maps and diagrams must link to their data or state that the asset is a schematic.
- Do not create invented pull quotes, composite quotations, fabricated citations or “expert” names.

## 7. Source integrity and generated material

**Never fabricate or infer a reference from a plausible URL.** Only list sources provided by the researcher or individually verified during research. For each reference, give its exact title, author/publisher, date, URL and the claim or context it supports when known. Mark missing metadata as “not verified”; do not fill it with a guess.

If source content is unavailable to the generator, make that limitation explicit. A provided URL alone does not establish what the page says. Distinguish a source recommendation that still needs review from an actually consulted source.

Likewise, the model must not generate “realistic data”, quotes, interviews, official statements, responses, access dates, dates, observations, citations or methodology results to make a story feel complete. Use explicit placeholders such as **SOURCE NEEDED**, **DATA REQUIRED**, **RESPONSE NOT YET OBTAINED**, or **UNVERIFIED** and route them for reporting.

## 8. Editor's checklist and release blockers

Score narrative craft from 0 to 2 for each dimension (0 = absent/unsafe, 1 = partial, 2 = strong):
- Central question and reader promise are clear.
- The chosen opening is evidence-led and appropriate to the format.
- Each section moves understanding forward; transitions are earned.
- Human experience and system explanation are proportionate and linked by evidence.
- Chronology and causal reasoning are accurate and distinct.
- Data adds a tested finding and explains its limits.
- Counterevidence and right of reply are handled fairly.
- The ending resolves the question or names what remains unknown.
- Language is precise, accessible and not emotionally manipulative.
- Visuals and captions are accurate, useful and transparently labelled.

**Score is diagnostic, not an automatic publication gate.** These are hard blockers regardless of score:
- An invented or untraceable material factual claim, quotation, source, statistic or scene detail.
- An allegation stated as a finding without sufficient evidence and appropriate review.
- A material source conflict, omitted counterevidence or unanswered right-of-reply requirement.
- A known material privacy, safety, rights or legal-risk concern not escalated.
- A misleading headline, chart, image, caption or sourcing claim.
- Missing human approval required by newsroom policy.

Writer and reviewer must record any blocker as **FAIL** or **NOT VERIFIED**. Do not polish around it; return the story for reporting, evidence or human decision.
